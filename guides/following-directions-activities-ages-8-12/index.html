Following Directions Activities for Kids Ages 8 to 12

By Arianna Andersen, Ph.D. · Updated 2026-11-10

Many capable children lose points not because they cannot do the work, but because they do not do what the question asked. They see a familiar-looking problem, race ahead, and miss the word "not," the second part of the instructions, or the request to show their work.

Why careless mistakes happen

Fast readers often skim. When a task looks like one they have seen before, they answer the question they expect instead of the one in front of them. Extra details, changing instructions, and multi-step tasks catch them out. This is a habit, and habits can be practiced.

A four-step routine: read, mark and count, do, check

  1. Read the whole task before starting.
  2. Mark and count each thing you are asked to do. Circle key words and number the steps.
  3. Do the work, one step at a time.
  4. Check that every part was done. Count the steps again.

Quick practice ideas

  • Write a three-step instruction for a simple task and hide a twist in step three.
  • Give a math word problem with an extra detail that is not needed, and ask your child which detail to ignore.
  • Ask your child to underline the exact question in a reading passage before answering it.

Make it a game, not a punishment

Children respond better when tricky directions feel like a puzzle to solve. Celebrate when your child catches the trick. Keep the academic content easy so the focus stays on careful reading, not on hard math.

When to talk to the school

If your child struggles with directions across many settings, or the problem seems linked to attention or reading difficulties, talk with the teacher or your child's doctor. Practice can help with habits, but it is not a substitute for an evaluation when one is needed.

Common questions

How often should my child practice?

One activity a day for a few minutes is enough to build the habit.

Does this work in a classroom?

Yes. The routine works at home, in class, or with a tutor.
