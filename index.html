Friendship practice at home

Choose the situation you want to practice.

These original parent guides give you a concrete next step. They are companion resources, not reproduced book pages, a diagnostic tool, or a promise that everyone will become friends.

Give other children room

A child may be excited to join a conversation and not notice how close they have moved. Start with one concrete action: step back and leave comfortable room. Ask the other person whether the distance is okay, and respect their answer. Practice with an adult rather than waiting until another child is upset. Different people need different amounts of space; the goal is responding respectfully, not measuring everyone against one rigid distance.

Practice one action: Step back and leave comfortable room. If they move away, respect the extra space.

Keep objects away from faces

When children are excited to share, the object can arrive before the question. Rehearse a different sequence: keep the object back, ask whether the other person wants to see it, and pause. An adult can model both an interested answer and a polite refusal. Respect either response. For objects that could hurt or startle someone, put safety first and get adult help when needed.

Practice one action: Hold it at a comfortable distance. Ask if they want to see it, then wait.

Ask before touching

Friendly intentions do not remove another person’s choice about touch. Practice asking before a hug, holding hands, or touching belongings that someone is using. Model accepting either answer without arguing. Offer an alternative greeting such as a wave, and make clear that the child’s own boundary matters too. The exercise is about consent and respect, not persuading someone to accept contact.

Practice one action: Ask before touching and respect the answer. A wave or a hello can be enough.

Different preferences without put-downs

Having different preferences is ordinary. Putting down another person’s enjoyment is a different action. Compare “That is not my favorite” with “That is stupid,” then ask how each might feel. Practice one respectful response and one genuine question. Do not make a child pretend to enjoy something; the replacement is respectful curiosity and taking turns, not forced agreement.

Practice one action: State your preference without insulting theirs. Ask what they like about it.

Wait for an opening

A child can have an interesting idea and still need to wait for an opening. Model a short pause, a response related to the current topic, and a request for a turn. Keep the first practice brief and let the adult demonstrate mistakes too. Distinguish ordinary conversation from urgent needs: danger, pain, or a safety concern should not wait for a perfect opening.

Practice one action: Pause until they finish, respond to what they said, and then ask for a turn.

Choose friends who choose you

Start by listening to the hurt instead of rushing to fix it. One missed invitation does not tell the whole story, so look at repeated patterns: shared invitations, willing play, listening, and care. Help the child consider another willing friend or a safe activity. Not Everyone Likes Pizza approaches exclusion and self-worth from a Christian perspective. Repeated cruelty, threats, or bullying require responsible adult support.

Practice one action: Notice willing friends and put effort into relationships that go both ways.

Answer before changing the topic

A related question is useful only if there is also room to answer it. Model responding to what the other person actually asked before introducing another topic. Switch roles, and let the child choose how to communicate. Keep feedback specific: “You answered my question” or “You stayed with what I said.” There can be several helpful responses, not one script to memorize.

Practice one action: Answer what was asked, add a related response, and leave room for another turn.

Ask about their interests

A child does not need to share every interest to show curiosity. Choose one detail the other person mentioned, ask a genuine question, and listen. Model a related response rather than a rapid series of questions. Take turns choosing the topic so both people’s interests matter. This practice supports participation without demanding a single personality or performance style.

Practice one action: Ask about their interest, listen to the answer, and respond before switching topics.

Ask before joining

Practice the whole sequence, not just the opener: ask to join, wait, and find out how the game is being played. An invitation is not permission to take over the rules. Model accepting a no without repeated pressure and looking for another safe activity or willing playmate. Adults should address bullying and unsafe exclusion rather than asking a child to solve every problem alone.

Practice one action: Ask to join, wait for the answer, and learn the game’s rules if invited.

Respect a no

Use a calm role-play with an adult. One person offers a game; the other politely declines. Rehearse a simple acceptance and another option, then switch roles. Make it clear that respecting a no is different from tolerating threats or unsafe treatment. Children can have boundaries themselves and ask responsible adults for help when needed.

Practice one action: Accept the answer and choose another option rather than trying to wear them down.

Take turns choosing

Choose two safe activities that both people are willing to consider. Practice making a shared plan or taking turns selecting one. An adult can model changing a plan and calmly hearing a different preference. Do not frame flexibility as doing anything someone asks: health, safety, sensory needs, and personal boundaries remain important. The useful skill is shared decision-making, not forced agreement.

Practice one action: Offer safe choices and take turns choosing together.

Tell about safety, not control

Teach this distinction carefully. The goal is not to silence children or make them responsible for sorting out harm alone. Danger, threats, bullying, injuries, and unsafe secrets deserve adult help. For small, safe disagreements, an adult can coach words and choices instead of putting a child in charge of policing peers. When a child is uncertain or distressed, asking a trusted adult is appropriate.

Practice one action: Ask an adult for help with danger, threats, bullying, or a problem you cannot safely handle.

Name the action, not the child

Choose one action rather than giving a general verdict on the child. Describe what happened, demonstrate a useful alternative, and switch roles. Specific feedback might be “You gave me time to answer” or “You asked about what I said.” Keep the activity short enough to be useful. The books are educational resources and do not promise that a particular exercise will produce a particular friendship outcome.

Practice one action: Name one action and model a specific replacement. Then rehearse it together.

Before you begin

Does this promise that everyone will like my child?

No. Friendship depends on both people. These books give adults and children concrete behaviors to discuss and practice; they do not guarantee acceptance or a particular result.

Can autistic children use these activities?

Adults can adapt the pace, wording, and practice to the individual child. The focus is on useful participation and reciprocal behavior, with respect for communication and sensory needs. These books are educational resources, not a diagnostic tool or a treatment program.

Does flexibility mean tolerating bullying?

No. Trying a safe game that is not your favorite is different from accepting danger, threats, or hurtful treatment. Children should seek adult help when there is danger or bullying.

How do I use the two books together?

Read the story and discuss its choices. Then select a related workbook activity, model the response, and practice it together. Keep sessions short enough for useful attention and feedback.
